Coefficient of Inbreeding

Breeding a pair of related animals produces more consistent, predictable traits in their offspring, but also causes harmful side effects: lower fertility, smaller offspring, early mortality, and shorter lifespan. The higher the level of inbreeding, the greater those detrimental effects.

The coefficient of inbreeding (F) estimates how inbred a potential offspring would be, as a percentage of its genes expected to be identical by descent (inherited as the same copy from an ancestor on both sides of its pedigree). The coefficient of relatedness (R) is a related measure of how closely the two parents themselves are related to each other.

Reference values

PairingCOI
Animal mated to its own parent25%
Full-sib matings (share both parents)25%
Half-sib matings (share one parent)12.5%
Single common great-grandparent3.1%
No known common ancestor0%

How it's calculated

This app computes the exact coefficient of kinship between the two selected birds using the standard recursive method (equivalent to Wright's path coefficient formula, summing (1/2)ⁿ over every shared ancestor's contribution) — the same value as the inbreeding coefficient their offspring would have.

More info: COI FAQs — Institute of Canine Biology

Mutation Predictor

Currently supports five species, each with its own set of genes: Green-Cheek Conure, Cockatiel, Indian Ringneck Parakeet, Peach-Faced Lovebird, and Gouldian Finch. Both selected birds must be the same species.

Kinds of genes involved

InheritanceWhat it means
RecessiveNeeds two copies to show; one copy is an invisible "split"
DominantShows with just one copy; a second copy usually looks the same
Incomplete dominantLooks different at 0, 1, and 2 copies — e.g. a lovebird's Dark Factor (Normal / Olive / Jade) or a cockatiel's Silver (Normal / Single Factor / Double Factor)
Sex-linked recessiveBirds are ZW, so males carry two copies and can be "split," while females carry only one and always show it if they have it — odds genuinely differ for sons vs. daughters, shown separately alongside the combined odds

What this can and can't know

A bird's spreadsheet entry only records what it looks like, not what recessive genes it silently carries ("splits"). This tool automatically detects proven splits from your pedigree — e.g. if two normal-looking parents produced a visibly mutated chick, whichever parent(s) could have hidden it must carry it. It can't detect a split that has never been proven by an offspring, so predictions are a floor: actual results can include more than shown if a parent turns out to carry an unproven split. For a bird showing a simple dominant trait (not incomplete dominant), whether it carries one or two copies is assumed to be one (the more conservative guess) unless proven otherwise.

Each species profile covers a solid, well-established core gene set for that species — not necessarily every mutation that exists for it.
