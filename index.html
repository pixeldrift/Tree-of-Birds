Coefficient of Inbreeding

Breeding a pair of related animals produces more consistent, predictable traits in their offspring, but also causes harmful side effects: lower fertility, smaller offspring, early mortality, and shorter lifespan. The higher the level of inbreeding, the greater those detrimental effects.

The coefficient of inbreeding (F) estimates how inbred a potential offspring would be, as a percentage of its genes expected to be identical by descent (inherited as the same copy from an ancestor on both sides of its pedigree). The coefficient of relatedness (R) is a related measure of how closely the two parents themselves are related to each other.

Reference values

PairingCOI
Animal mated to its own parent25%
Full-sib matings (share both parents)25%
Half-sib matings (share one parent)12.5%
Single common great-grandparent3.1%
No known common ancestor0%

How it's calculated

This app computes the exact coefficient of kinship between the two selected birds using the standard recursive method (equivalent to Wright's path coefficient formula, summing (1/2)ⁿ over every shared ancestor's contribution) — the same value as the inbreeding coefficient their offspring would have.

More info: COI FAQs — Institute of Canine Biology

Mutation Predictor

Currently supports Green-Cheek Conures only. Four genes control their color mutations — two ordinary recessive genes and two sex-linked recessive genes (birds are ZW, so males carry two copies and can be "split" carriers, while females carry only one copy and always show a sex-linked trait if they have it at all):

GeneInheritance
DiluteRecessive
TurquoiseRecessive
OpalineSex-linked recessive
CinnamonSex-linked recessive

The 16 combinations of these four genes produce the named mutations bred in this variety: Normal, Turquoise, Cinnamon, Turquoise Cinnamon, Yellow-Sided, Turquoise Yellow-Sided, Pineapple, Turquoise Cinnamon Yellow-Sided, Dilute, Mint, Dilute Cinnamon, Cinnamint, Dilute Yellow-Sided, Opamint, Suncheek, and Mooncheek.

What this can and can't know

A bird's spreadsheet entry only records what it looks like, not what recessive genes it silently carries ("splits"). This tool automatically detects proven splits from your pedigree — e.g. if two normal-looking parents produced a visibly Cinnamon chick, both parents (or just the father, for a sex-linked gene) must carry it. It can't detect a split that has never been proven by an offspring, so predictions are a floor: actual results can include additional mutations if a parent turns out to carry an unproven split.

Because two of the four genes are sex-linked, exact odds can differ for sons vs. daughters — both are shown alongside the combined odds.
